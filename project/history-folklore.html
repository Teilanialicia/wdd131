The Mystery of The Once Lost Borobudur

The Borobudur Temple is one of The Seven Wonders of the World. The Borobudur is not one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, only because it was unknown at the time the list for the Ancient World was created.

Borobudur was constructed between about 778 and 850 ce, under the Shailendra dynasty. At some point in history it was abandoned, the reasons for which are still unknown. Through the centuries it became buried under volcanic ash and jungle vegetation until discovered by English lieutenant governor Thomas Stamford Raffles in 1814. Since then multiple restoration have been made to support the structure of the lost Borobudur. One most recently done by UNESCO and the Indonesian Goverment in 1970-1980s included dismantling and rebuilding the enitre structure.

A People Lost to History

Fossilised remains of Homo erectus, popularly known as the "Java Man", dating back 1.3 million years were found along the banks of the Bengawan Solo River.

H. erectus arrived in Eurasia approximately 1.8 million years ago, in an event considered to be the first African exodus. There is evidence that the Java population of H. erectus lived in an ever-wet forest habitat. More specifically the environment resembled a savannah, but was likely regularly inundated ("hydromorphic savanna"). The plants found at the Trinil excavation site included grass (Poaceae), ferns, Ficus, and Indigofera, which are typical of lowland rainforest.

H. e. soloensis was the last population of a long occupation history of the island of Java by H. erectus, beginning 1.51 to 0.93 million years ago at the Sangiran site, continuing 540 to 430 thousand years ago at the Trinil site, and finally 117 to 108 thousand years ago at Ngandong. If the date is correct for Solo Man, then they would represent a terminal population of H. erectus which sheltered in the last open-habitat refuges of East Asia before the rainforest takeover.

Javan Mouse-Deer

With an average length of 45 cm (18 in) and an average height of 30 cm (12 in), the Java mouse-deer is the smallest extant (living) ungulate or hoofed mammal, as well as the smallest extant even-toed ungulate.

The weight of the Java mouse-deer ranges from 1 to 2 kilograms (2.2 to 4.4 lb), with males being heavier than females. It has an average tail length of about 5 cm (2.0 in). Mouse-deer are thought to be the most primitive ruminants based on their behaviour and the fossil record, thus they are the living link between ruminants and non-ruminants.

The Java mouse-deer is endothermic and homoeothermic, and has an average basal metabolic rate of about 4.883 watts. It also has the smallest red blood cells (erythrocytes) of any mammal, and about 12.8% of the cells have pits on them. The pits range in diameter from 68 to 390 nanometres. Red blood cells with pits are unique and have not been reported before either physiologically or pathologically.

Historically, the mouse-deer has featured prominently in Malay and Indonesian folklore, where it is considered a wise creature. This character, Sang Kancil (pronounced “Kahn-cheel”), is a diminutive but wise mouse-deer. Sang Kancil is a tiny and cunning hero who, through his intelligence, is able to prevail over his larger tyrants and foes.

Religion

Indonesia's original religions came to Indonesia through the silk trade with India in the 1st century. Hinduism was the first religion and Buddhism the second. Hinduism came to Indonesia in the 1st-century through Indian traders, merchants, sailors, etc. doing trade with the local peoples. Buddhism also came to Indonesia in the 2nd century through the same means

Because Indonesia has strong cultural influence from both religions, most of Indonesian kingdoms were Hindu-Buddhist kingdoms, as evidenced by oral histories and deities as well as ancient surviving temples and architectural landmarks.

Majapahit was one of the last major Hindu-Buddhist empires of the region and is considered to be one of the greatest and most powerful empires in the history of Indonesia and Southeast Asia. It is sometimes seen as the precedent for Indonesia's modern boundaries.

Queen Tribhuwana

Tribhuwana Tunggadewi (r. 1328 to 1350) was a significant Javanese queen of the Majapahit Empire, the last major Hindu-Buddhist kingdom in Indonesia. Born Dyah Gitarja, she was the daughter of Raden Wijaya, the empire's founder, and his consort Dyah Gayatri, making her a key figure in the royal lineage. Her half-brother, Jayanagara, became king upon their father's death in 1309 but confined Tribhuwana and her sister, Dyah Wiyat, to prevent them from marrying and potentially producing rivals.

After Jayanagara's assassination in 1328—allegedly orchestrated by Gajah Mada, a loyal advisor seeking to liberate the sisters—Tribhuwana married Cakradhara, Duke of Tumapel, and ascended the throne as queen, taking the regnal name Tribhuwana Tunggadewi Jayawishnuwardhani.

Under her reign, she led military campaigns, including quelling rebellions in Sadeng and Keta, and appointed Gajah Mada as prime minister, who vowed to expand Majapahit's influence across the archipelago. Despite facing opposition, including a duel with a rival, her leadership facilitated significant territorial expansion.

Tribhuwana was noted for her valor, wisdom, and intelligence. Her reign concluded with her mother’s death in 1350, leading her to abdicate in favor of her son, Hayam Wuruk, though she remained active in state affairs, later serving as ruler of the Kahuripan region and a member of the royal council.

Upon her death, she was cremated in a grand ceremony and posthumously deified as the goddess Parvati at Rimbi temple, East Java, symbolizing her enduring legacy in Indonesian culture and spirituality.

Significance of White Tigers

White tigers are significant to the Sundanese culture and play a part in both symbolism and local oral folklore

Some key culturally significant aspects of white tigers to the Sundanese are:

Symbol of Power: White tigers are often associated with royal authority and bravery. They embody the qualities of a strong leader, making them a fitting emblem for figures like King Siliwangi.

Cultural Myths: In local legends, white tigers are portrayed as mystical beings or guardians that accompany noble figures. Their presence in stories reinforces the connection between the natural and supernatural worlds.

Protection: The tigers are seen as protectors of the land and its people. Their fierce nature symbolizes the safeguarding of the community against external threats.

Spiritual Significance: White tigers are sometimes considered messengers or embodiments of ancestral spirits, linking the living with the spiritual realm.

Significance of Rice to the Javanese, Balinese, Indonesian and South East Asian Peoples

Rice holds profound cultural, economic, and spiritual significance for both the Javanese and Balinese people. Here are some key aspects of its importance:

Staple Food: Rice is a primary staple in the diets of both Javanese and Balinese people, forming the basis of most meals. It is central to their culinary traditions. Symbol of Life and Prosperity: Rice symbolizes life, fertility, and abundance. It is often associated with prosperity and is integral to rituals and celebrations.
Cultural Rituals: Both cultures include rice in various ceremonies and rituals, such as weddings, harvest festivals, and religious offerings. For instance, the Balinese celebrate the "Nganyarin" ceremony to honor rice and the spirits of the fields.

Dewi Sri; The Rice Goddess

Dewi Sri Dewi Sri is the Indonesian Rice Goddess, originating on the Island of Java, equivalent of the Indian Goddess Lakshmi and often regarded as an incarnation or one of her manifestations. The goddess is also associated with wealth and prosperity. Sri is the Goddess of the earth and the Mother of the Javanese people.

In the Javanese harvest ceremonies She is worshiped as the Rice Mother and as the Rice Bride. She is widely venerated as the great benefactress of the people who protects them against hunger, even in the semi-Islamized regions. Sri brings the rain when the monsoon arrives and appears in dreams to give good advice. She is unique in the Balinese pantheon for She is the only member of the principal deities who did not originate in Indian Hinduism. She is the consort of Wisnu, the God of Sustenance. Dewi Sri is considered to be the inspirer of Bali’s highly productive method of cooperative rice farming as well as Bali’s master landscaper. She protects and nurtures the rice padi and the farmers pray to her for bountiful harvest.


Symbol of Fertility and Abundance Dewi Sri is revered as the goddess of rice and fertility. Her association with rice underscores its importance as a staple food that sustains life. The veneration of Dewi Sri reflects the people's reliance on agriculture for survival and prosperity.

Cultural Rituals and Ceremonies Many traditional rituals and ceremonies dedicated to rice include offerings to Dewi Sri. For instance, harvest festivals in both Bali and Java often involve prayers and offerings to honor her, seeking her blessings for a bountiful harvest and the well-being of the community.

Spiritual Protection Dewi Sri is believed to protect rice fields and farmers. Farmers often invoke her name to ensure good weather, pest control, and overall successful cultivation. This spiritual aspect reinforces the deep connection between agricultural practices and divine favor.

Cultural Identity The goddess is a significant figure in the cultural narratives of Indonesia, symbolizing not only agricultural abundance but also the harmony between humans and nature. She embodies the ideals of community, care for the land, and respect for the natural cycles that sustain life.

Art and Representation Dewi Sri is often depicted in various forms of art, textiles, and traditional performances across Indonesia, celebrating her role as a nurturer and protector. This representation highlights the cultural significance of rice and its divine connection.

Dewi Sri and The Land

Dewi Sri, the Indonesian goddess of rice and fertility, is closely associated with the concept of fertile land, particularly the rich volcanic soil found in many parts of Indonesia. Here’s how this connection plays out:

Fertile Volcanic Soil Ideal Conditions for Rice: Indonesia's volcanic earth is highly fertile, providing the essential nutrients needed for rice cultivation. This soil results from volcanic eruptions, which enrich the land with minerals that enhance agricultural productivity. Agricultural Abundance: The fertility of volcanic soil supports diverse crops, especially rice, which thrives in these conditions. The presence of Dewi Sri symbolizes the blessings of such fertile land.

Cultural Symbolism Representation of Life: Dewi Sri embodies not just rice itself but also the life-giving properties of the earth. The fertility of the land is seen as a manifestation of her blessings, emphasizing the interconnectedness between the goddess and the agricultural landscape. Nurturing Nature: In many traditions, Dewi Sri is viewed as a nurturing figure, protecting the land and ensuring that it remains productive. Her association with fertile soil reinforces the idea that agricultural success is linked to divine favor.

Rituals and Practices Ceremonial Offerings: Farmers often perform rituals to honor Dewi Sri, especially before planting and during harvests. These rituals aim to seek her protection and blessings for a bountiful crop, reflecting the importance of soil health and fertility in their agricultural practices. Seasonal Festivals: Festivals celebrating the harvest typically include expressions of gratitude to Dewi Sri for the fertile land that sustains their crops. These events highlight the deep respect for both the goddess and the natural resources.

Sustainable Practices Traditional Knowledge: The understanding of volcanic soil’s fertility is often intertwined with local agricultural wisdom. The reverence for Dewi Sri encourages sustainable farming practices that honor the land and its ability to provide. Community Bonding: The rituals surrounding rice cultivation foster community bonds, with collective efforts to care for the land and honor Dewi Sri, reinforcing the cultural significance of agriculture in everyday life.

Dewi Sris association with fertile volcanic soil underscores the vital relationship between agriculture, spirituality, and environmental stewardship in Indonesian culture. Her presence symbolizes not only the fertility of the land necessary for rice production but also the deep respect for the natural world that sustains life. This connection enriches the cultural practices of communities across Indonesia, reflecting their dependence on and reverence for both the goddess and the land.

Demographics

Indonesia is made up of over 13,000 islands, and its many ethnic groups are characterized by different languages and cultural practices. The three largest ethnic groups of Indonesia are, in order: Javanese, Sundanese and the Malay.

Javanese

The Javanese constitute Indonesia’s largest ethnic group, accounting for roughly one-third of the total population. Most Javanese live in the densely settled, irrigated agricultural regions of central and eastern Java—the most populous parts of the country. The cities of Yogyakarta and Surakarta (Solo), in the centre of the island, are strongholds of Javanese culture and maintain traditional rulers, although these leaders have no real political power.(Britannica)

Sundanese

The Sundanese are an indigenous ethnic group native to the western region of Java island in Indonesia, primarily West Java. They number approximately 42 million and form Indonesia's second most populous ethnic group. They speak the Sundanese language, which is part of the Austronesian languages. Java’s western region, including the city of Bandung, is the homeland of the Sundanese, who are related to but quite distinct from the Javanese in language and tradition. The Sundanese are the second largest ethnic group in Indonesia.(Wikipedia)
